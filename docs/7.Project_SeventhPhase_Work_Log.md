# Seventh Phase Work Log: EF-001 and EF-002

## Context

This phase follows Derek's observation that EF-001 contains variable night-time
beacon signals. During sleep, the bracelet movement signal remains low, but the
room assigned from the strongest RSSI repeatedly switches to adjacent rooms.

The aim is therefore to use movement as temporal evidence:

```text
low movement over a long period -> room changes are unlikely
```

RSSI remains the spatial signal. ACC is used to decide when RSSI room switching
should be treated as plausible movement and when it should be treated as likely
signal instability.

The complete conceptual workflow is summarised below.

![Movement-supported RSSI room-correction conceptual workflow](../Results/SeventhPhase/SeventhPhase_movement_supported_rssi_workflow.svg)

**Figure take-home message.** ACC and RSSI are processed independently at the
same fixed 5-minute resolution before being combined. RSSI supplies the proposed
room, while the ACC-derived movement context determines whether rapid changes in
that proposal are plausible. The final correction is deliberately conservative:
ACC never assigns a room by itself, and unsupported RSSI gaps remain missing.

The two signals have deliberately separate roles. RSSI proposes *where* the
participant is; ACC provides evidence about *whether rapid changes in that room
proposal are physically plausible*. ACC is not used to name a room, and RSSI is
not used to decide whether the participant is moving.

### 2.1 Intuitive Meaning of the ACC and RSSI Features

For every accelerometer sample, the three axes are reduced to an orientation-
independent magnitude:

```text
m(t) = sqrt(ax(t)^2 + ay(t)^2 + az(t)^2)
```

Magnitude is preferable to inspecting only one axis because a wrist rotation
can redistribute acceleration among `x`, `y`, and `z` even if the overall level
of movement is similar. Samples above `1.2` are clipped before calculating the
window statistics, following the existing Sixth Phase spike-cleaning convention.

Two summaries can then be calculated within a 5-minute window:

```text
mean magnitude = average level of m(t)
magnitude std  = within-window variability of m(t)
```

These summaries do not have the same interpretation. In a stationary wearable,
the magnitude mean remains close to the approximately constant gravity-related
baseline. It may therefore remain near the same value during both still and
active windows. The magnitude standard deviation measures how much the signal
fluctuates around its local mean, so wrist movement, vibration, and changing
dynamic acceleration generally increase it.

This distinction is visible in the EF datasets:

| Dataset | Median magnitude mean: low / higher motion | Median magnitude std: low / higher motion |
|---|---:|---:|
| EF-001 | 1.0069 / 1.0118 | 0.00293 / 0.08380 |
| EF-002 | 1.0033 / 1.0090 | 0.00675 / 0.05693 |

The magnitude mean changes only slightly between the threshold-defined groups,
whereas the magnitude standard deviation has much clearer separation. This is
descriptive rather than independent validation because the groups themselves
are defined from standard deviation, but it confirms that the magnitude mean
does not provide an equivalent separation in these data. This, together with
the gravity-baseline interpretation, is why standard deviation was retained as
the working movement feature. It should
nevertheless be described as a proxy for *signal stability*, not as a direct
measurement of sleep or stillness: smooth constant motion can have a low
standard deviation, sensor noise can raise it, and a quiet awake period can look
similar to sleep. The later duration and continuity rules are needed because a
single low-variability window is insufficient evidence of sleep.

For RSSI, each beacon is summarised separately:

```text
mean_RSSI(beacon, window) = average RSSI received from that beacon in 5 minutes
raw room(window) = room of argmax_beacon mean_RSSI(beacon, window)
```

The per-beacon mean reduces sample-level radio noise and indicates relative
proximity, but the transition of interest is a change in the derived strongest-
beacon label:

```text
transition(k) = 1 if raw_room(k) != raw_room(k-1), otherwise 0
```

This transition is evaluated only for contiguous RSSI-observed windows. During
a long low-variability ACC episode, repeated label changes without corresponding
movement are physiologically implausible and are treated as evidence of beacon
competition or propagation instability rather than repeated room changes.

### 2.2 Direct Alignment of Room Transitions and ACC Variability

To address the supervisor's suggestion directly, raw strongest-room transition
events were aligned with the continuous 5-minute ACC magnitude standard
deviation. A transition is counted only when two consecutive 5-minute windows
are contiguous, both contain RSSI observations, and their strongest-beacon room
labels differ. Each event is then classified using the ACC standard deviation
of the new room-label window:

```text
red transition line    = raw room changed while ACC std <= 0.023
orange transition line = raw room changed while ACC std > 0.023
```

The detailed example was not selected manually. The script calculated the
transition count for every main-sleep candidate and selected the episode with
the largest raw count. This was EF-001 Episode 1, from 19 June 00:10 to 09:35:

| Transition measure within Episode 1 | Count |
|---|---:|
| Raw room transitions | 37 |
| Raw transitions during low motion | 33 |
| Raw transitions during higher motion | 4 |
| Corrected room transitions | 0 |

## 3. Data-Derived ACC Low-Motion Threshold

The Sixth Phase exploratory sleep analysis used:

```text
ACC magnitude std <= 0.010
```

For EF-001, this threshold was too low and represented near-complete stillness
rather than the wider low-motion platform associated with sleep.

I therefore clustered the logarithm of the 5-minute cleaned ACC magnitude
standard deviation into four motion states. The cluster centres were:

| Motion state | ACC std centre |
|---|---:|
| Near-still | 0.00288 |
| Quiet / small movement | 0.01379 |
| Transitional movement | 0.03830 |
| Clear activity | 0.09552 |

The log-space boundary between the quiet and transitional states was:

```text
0.02298, reported as 0.023
```

The working low-motion rule is therefore:

```text
low motion = 5-minute cleaned ACC magnitude std <= 0.023
```

![EF-001 ACC motion-state clustering](../Results/SeventhPhase/EF-001/EF-001_acc_threshold_clusters.png)

This threshold is EF-001-specific and should not yet be treated as a universal
sleep threshold.

### 3.1 What the Log-Space Boundary Means

ACC standard deviations are positive and strongly right-skewed: the distance
from `0.003` to `0.006` is more naturally interpreted as a doubling than as an
absolute increase of `0.003`. Clustering was therefore performed on
`log10(ACC std)`, where equal distances represent equal ratios on the original
scale. Four clusters were used as an exploratory description of near-still,
quiet, transitional, and clearly active windows; they are not supervised
activity labels.

After clustering, the dividing point between two adjacent log-space centres is
their midpoint in log units. When transformed back, this is the geometric mean
of the two original-scale centres:

```text
boundary = 10^((log10(c1) + log10(c2)) / 2) = sqrt(c1 * c2)
```

For the EF-001 quiet and transitional centres:

```text
sqrt(0.01379 * 0.03830) = 0.02298 ~= 0.023
```

Thus, a “log-space boundary” is not an additional manually chosen threshold; it
is the equal-distance decision point between two fitted centres on the scale
used for clustering. The choice of cluster count and the interpretation of the
clusters remain exploratory and require checking on additional participants.

## 4. Initial Movement-Adaptive RSSI Tests

The Fourth and Fifth Phase adaptive-window idea was used as the starting point,
but it was not copied directly. EF-001 has one participant, no step-count signal
for this analysis, and a specific night-time beacon-instability problem.

The adapted approach was:

1. Use 5-minute mean RSSI to obtain the raw strongest beacon.
2. Keep the raw room during higher movement.
3. During low movement, take a majority vote over the strongest beacon labels in
   a longer centred window.
4. Resolve vote ties using mean RSSI within the long window.
5. Keep missing RSSI missing and never vote across a data gap.

A 30-minute window reduced low-motion room transitions from 252 to 84. Increasing
the window to 60 minutes reduced them further to 51. However, a moving window
still changed membership over time, so its winning room could continue to
switch during a long sleep period.

### 4.1 Fixed, Sliding, and Episode-Level Windows

Three different forms of temporal aggregation are used, and they serve different
purposes:

| Window type | Current use | Benefit | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed, non-overlapping 5-minute windows | ACC statistics and per-beacon mean RSSI | Gives one aligned and auditable feature row per time interval; avoids counting each raw sample in many base windows | A movement or room change near a boundary is divided between two windows; temporal precision is limited to 5 minutes |
| Centred sliding 30- or 60-minute window, updated every 5 minutes | Initial low-motion majority-vote correction | Uses more neighbouring evidence and suppresses isolated RSSI label changes | Adjacent outputs share most observations; membership changes at every step and can move the winning room; a centred window also uses future data and is therefore an offline, non-causal method |
| Variable-duration episode | Final long low-motion correction | Maintains one consistent room hypothesis across a complete sleep-like period and tolerates brief movement | Depends on episode-boundary and duration assumptions; the result is available only after sufficient episode context is observed |

The fixed 5-minute windows are the common base resolution rather than a claim
that all relevant behaviour lasts five minutes. The sliding window was useful as
an intermediate baseline, but its changing membership explains why increasing
it from 30 to 60 minutes reduced rather than eliminated room switching. For the
offline night-time analysis, an episode-level decision better matches the
conceptual assumption: if movement remains low across one sleep-like episode,
the participant is unlikely to move repeatedly between rooms within that
episode. A future real-time implementation would need a trailing rather than a
centred window, or would delay its decision until an episode ends.

## 5. Why Low-Motion Windows Were Still Fragmented

A low-motion window is not the same as a sleep episode. Short periods above the
ACC threshold can occur during turning, briefly getting up, or other sleep-time
movement. Treating every 5-minute threshold crossing as an episode boundary
therefore split visually continuous sleep platforms.

For example, the initial segmentation produced two same-night fragments:

```text
00:30-03:45 = 195 minutes
04:00-08:15 = 255 minutes
```

and another pair:

```text
00:20-03:15 = 175 minutes
03:30-09:20 = 350 minutes
```

The two parts of each pair were separated by only 15 minutes of non-low-motion
data. They are more plausibly interpreted as one sleep period containing a
brief interruption.

## 6. All-Day Low-Motion Episode Clustering

To avoid assuming a fixed `18:00-10:00` night range, candidate episodes are now
detected across the full 24-hour timeline.

The current candidate construction is:

```text
low-motion threshold = ACC std <= 0.023
minimum candidate duration = 60 minutes
allowed non-low-motion interruption = 15 minutes
minimum low-motion share for a selected main-sleep episode = 60%
```

Fifteen minutes was selected as the smallest tolerance that merged the clearly
split same-night periods. Wider 20-30 minute tolerances were not used because
they increasingly risk merging sleep with separate pre-sleep or post-sleep
rest.

The all-day candidate durations were divided into two clusters. With the updated
episode construction, the centres were approximately:

| Cluster | Duration centre |
|---|---:|
| Short rest | 111 minutes |
| Main sleep | 559 minutes |

The log-space centre boundary was approximately 249 minutes. This numerical
boundary should not be over-interpreted: the longest short-rest candidate was
approximately 195 minutes and the shortest main-sleep candidate was 465
minutes, leaving a clear empty interval. Any boundary inside that interval
would give the same classification. Linear- and log-duration two-cluster
solutions also produced the same 10 short-rest and 12 main-sleep grouping.

The count of 12 main-sleep candidates was an output of the current clustering,
not a target imposed because 12 periods were visible by eye.

The duration boundary is calculated in the same way as the ACC boundary. The
episode-duration centres are fitted after taking the natural logarithm, and the
midpoint is transformed back to minutes. Consequently, the boundary is the
geometric mean of the short-rest and main-sleep centres:

```text
sqrt(111 minutes * 559 minutes) ~= 249 minutes
```

Log duration is used because bout durations are positive, right-skewed, and
better compared by ratios than by absolute differences. In EF-001, the empty
195- to 465-minute interval is stronger evidence for the separation than the
exact value `249`; moving the threshold within that empty interval would not
change any episode label.

![EF-001 all-day low-motion episode clustering](../Results/SeventhPhase/EF-001/EF-001_all_day_low_motion_episode_clusters.png)

The selected episodes had a padded descriptive clock range of approximately
20:26-11:26. This range is reported after episode detection; it is not used to
select the episodes.

## 7. Episode-Level Dominant Room Correction

For each selected long low-motion episode, the raw 5-minute strongest-beacon
labels are counted across the complete episode. The most frequent beacon is
used as the episode-level dominant room. If the count is tied, the mean RSSI
within the episode is used as supporting evidence.

All observed windows inside the episode are assigned to this dominant room.
Short movement interruptions are retained as part of the same episode, while
RSSI data gaps remain missing. Outside the selected episodes, the 60-minute
low-motion vote remains the adaptive method.

The original full-record comparison is retained as an overview. Following
supervisor feedback on 12 July, the report-facing comparison is now split into
aligned 24-hour local-calendar-day panels. Each dated panel uses the full page
width and places the raw and corrected room sequences directly above one
another. A thin dark-blue bar marks the selected main-sleep episode. This makes
short, implausible raw room changes visible rather than compressing nearly two
weeks into one horizontal strip.

The daily comparison plot contains:

* raw 5-minute strongest-RSSI room;
* movement-supported corrected room;
* a thin main-sleep marker below the paired room bars.

![Daily raw RSSI room versus episode-level corrected room](../Results/SeventhPhase/EF-001/EF-001_daily_raw_vs_low_motion_adaptive_rssi.png)

**Figure take-home message.** Within the dark-blue main-sleep intervals, the raw

## 9. Validation and Interpretation

The current tests cover majority voting, RSSI tie-breaking, high-motion fallback,
missing RSSI, continuous-segment boundaries, brief motion interruption, and
episode-level dominant-room locking.

The full-data checks confirmed:

* high-motion windows outside selected sleep episodes are not changed by the
  moving-window correction;
* missing RSSI windows are not filled;
* long-window voting does not cross observed-data gaps;
* short motion interruptions can remain inside a long sleep episode and inherit
  that episode's dominant room.

This remains a movement-supported RSSI correction rather than a ground-truth
location result. The algorithm assumes that room changes are unlikely during a
long low-motion episode. It does not prove that every selected episode is sleep
or that its dominant beacon is the true room.

Important current limitations are:

* EF-001 contains only a small number of nights;
* the two-cluster duration model is exploratory;
* the 15-minute interruption tolerance should be checked on additional homes;
* one episode is Living-dominant, which should not be changed to Bedroom without
  independent evidence;
* one episode has no RSSI, so its room cannot be inferred from the available
  beacon data.

## Current Seventh Phase Conclusion

EF-001 supports the central hypothesis that movement can help distinguish
plausible room transitions from RSSI instability. A simple moving RSSI window
reduced low-motion switching, but an episode-level method was more suitable for
long sleep-like platforms. The current pipeline derives both the ACC threshold
and the long-duration episode class from EF-001 data, while explicitly retaining
uncertainty where RSSI is missing or where a non-Bedroom beacon dominates.

EF-002 will be used to test whether these findings generalise or whether the
thresholds and episode rules need to remain participant- or home-specific.
