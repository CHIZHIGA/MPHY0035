## Part 1: Pressure-Based Floor Inference

### Aim

The aim of this part is to test whether the pressure sensors can provide useful floor-level information. Both the bracelet and the environmental beacons contain pressure sensors. Since air pressure decreases with height, beacons on different floors should have different pressure baselines, and the bracelet pressure should be closer to the beacons on the same floor.

This is different from room-level RSSI localisation. Pressure is not expected to identify the room directly, but it can provide a vertical-location constraint:

```text
pressure -> floor level
RSSI -> room or beacon proximity within the floor
movement -> stability / transition context
```

### Pressure Cleaning

The raw pressure data contained several obvious artefacts:

- very short single-point or short-run spikes;
- longer bracelet pressure plateaus that were inconsistent with all beacon pressure traces;
- short residual bracelet offset segments.

The cleaning strategy was deliberately conservative:

1. Correct long bracelet pressure plateaus using the beacon median pressure as a local baseline.
2. Correct short offset segments after the long plateau correction.
3. Keep the cleaned pressure continuous rather than introducing missing gaps where possible.
4. Use diagnostic plots to compare pressure cleaning with the resulting floor timeline.

### Same-Floor Pressure Baseline

A simple zero pressure-difference line is not the best same-floor baseline, because the bracelet is worn at body height rather than at the exact height of the beacon pressure sensor.

To estimate the same-floor baseline, I used a robust one-dimensional two-centre estimate on the bracelet-minus-floor-group pressure differences:

1. Compute bracelet pressure minus each floor-group median pressure.
2. Pool these pressure differences.
3. Fit a robust 1D two-centre k-medians model.
4. Treat the centre closest to zero as the same-floor baseline.

Result:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---:|
| Same-floor baseline | -0.053 hPa |
| Nearest-group median cross-check | -0.062 hPa |
| 95% bootstrap CI for nearest-group median | -0.066 to -0.057 hPa |
| Estimated floor pressure gap | 0.384 hPa |
| Approximate floor height | 3.19 m |

This supports the interpretation that the same-floor line should be slightly below zero rather than exactly zero.

Key output:

![Bracelet-beacon pressure difference baseline](../Results/SixthPhase/NewData80h/new80h_bracelet_beacon_pressure_difference_same_floor_baseline.png)

### Floor Timeline Method

For each 5-minute window:

1. Compute bracelet pressure minus the median pressure of the 1F beacon group.
2. Compute bracelet pressure minus the median pressure of the 2F beacon group.
3. Compare both values with the same-floor baseline of approximately `-0.053 hPa`.
4. Assign the floor whose pressure difference is closer to the same-floor baseline.
5. Apply a small smoothing step to remove very short isolated floor flips.

The decision rule is:

```text
if abs(bracelet_minus_1F_group - same_floor_baseline)
   < abs(bracelet_minus_2F_group - same_floor_baseline):
       inferred_floor = 1F
else:
       inferred_floor = 2F
```

The output also includes a pressure-floor confidence score based on the decision margin between the closest and second-closest floor distances.

### Environmental Beacon Gaps and Out-of-Home Masking

Participant feedback later identified two periods when the user was out of the
home. The raw wearable streams (`PRESSURE` and `HE_ACC`) remain available during
these periods, but the home beacon pressure and beacon RSSI streams are absent.
They should therefore be interpreted as **environmental-beacon absence**, not
as missing wearable data or a floor transition.

The updated pipeline now creates a complete 5-minute timeline and marks every
window without environmental beacon observations as unavailable. It applies two
rules:

```text
no beacon pressure/RSSI observation -> no inferred floor label
floor or room transition -> valid only between adjacent observed 5-minute windows
```

This prevents smoothing, stair-event detection, room-transition counting, and
long-stay episode construction from connecting the last indoor observation to
the first observation after an absence.

The long-gap audit is saved in:

```text
Results/SixthPhase/NewData80h/new80h_long_environmental_data_gaps.csv
```

| Gap | UTC interval without beacon observations | Duration | Interpretation |
|---|---|---:|---|
| `out_of_home_1` | 2026-06-16 11:40-13:40 | 125 min | Participant-confirmed out-of-home period |
| `long_environmental_gap_2` | 2026-06-18 07:50-09:10 | 85 min | Long unexplained environmental beacon gap; excluded but not labelled out-of-home |
| `out_of_home_2` | 2026-06-18 15:45-19:50 | 250 min | Participant-confirmed out-of-home period |

The two confirmed out-of-home intervals account for 375 minutes (6.25 hours)
of environmental-beacon absence. Across all long and short gaps, 113 of the
961 five-minute windows have no beacon observation, leaving 848 observed
environmental windows (70.67 hours) for pressure-floor and RSSI analysis.

As a validation check, six beacon-label changes would have appeared when
comparing observations across a data gap. They are now excluded from the room
transition metrics, and no pressure-floor shift is allowed at a gap boundary.

### Interpretation

The pressure data provide a useful and interpretable floor-level signal. The two beacon pressure groups are clearly separated, and the estimated pressure gap is consistent with a plausible one-floor height difference.

The pressure-only floor timeline should not be treated as a complete location estimate. It gives vertical location only. However, it can constrain the RSSI-based room estimate by identifying which floor the user is likely to be on. This should be useful when RSSI signals from beacons on different floors are ambiguous or when a strong beacon signal could be misleading because of signal propagation across floors.

Some short floor shifts appear close to residual bracelet pressure spikes. These should be treated cautiously and may need further cleaning or confidence filtering. Longer floor segments are more likely to reflect sustained pressure differences rather than isolated filtering artefacts.

## Part 2: ACC Support for Pressure Floor Changes

### Aim

The aim of this part is to test whether pressure-derived floor changes are consistent with movement evidence from the bracelet accelerometer. The assumption is that a genuine floor transition should usually involve some movement, whereas a pressure-only floor shift with no ACC support may be a lower-confidence candidate or a possible residual pressure artefact.

ACC is used here as supporting evidence only. It is not used to directly override the pressure-derived floor label.

### Script

```text

```

Run command:

```bash

```

The ACC analysis uses:


### ACC Cleaning and Feature Extraction

The raw ACC magnitude contains very few large spikes. I treated values above `1.2` as isolated spikes and replaced them with `1.0` for the cleaned ACC features, while keeping the raw spike count as a diagnostic feature.

The 5-minute ACC features are:

```text
acc_magnitude_mean_clean
acc_magnitude_std_clean
acc_magnitude_max_raw
acc_spike_count_gt_1p2
acc_motion_score
```

The `acc_motion_score` is currently defined as:

```text
acc_motion_score = acc_magnitude_std_clean
```

The ACC support threshold was selected empirically from the current data. Across all 5-minute windows, the ACC magnitude standard deviation was usually low, while pressure floor shift windows had noticeably higher variability. The initial support threshold was therefore set to:

```text
acc_magnitude_std_clean >= 0.010
```

This threshold is not treated as a final physiological threshold. It is a first data-driven threshold for identifying whether a pressure-derived floor transition has visible movement support.

### Shift Support Rule

A pressure floor shift is defined as a change in the smoothed pressure-derived floor label.

For each shift, the script checks the shift window and one neighbouring 5-minute window on each side:

```text
shift_time +/- 5 minutes
```

A shift is marked as ACC-supported if either:

```text
max acc_magnitude_std_clean in the support window >= 0.010
```

or:

```text
raw ACC spike count > 0
```

Otherwise, the shift is marked as:

```text
unsupported_floor_shift_candidate
```

### Interpretation

The ACC analysis supports the pressure-derived floor timeline. Most pressure floor shifts are accompanied by increased ACC variability within a small time window. This suggests that the pressure-derived floor changes are generally consistent with real movement rather than being mainly caused by residual pressure artefacts.

The conclusion should still be framed conservatively:

> Pressure-derived floor changes are generally consistent with movement evidence from the bracelet accelerometer. Most floor transitions coincide with increased ACC variability, supporting the pressure floor timeline as a plausible vertical-location signal. A small number of floor shifts do not have clear ACC support and should be treated as lower-confidence candidates.

Therefore, the pressure floor timeline can be treated as the main floor-level signal, while ACC provides transition confidence and helps identify possible artefact candidates.

## Part 3: Combine Pressure Floor With RSSI

The next step was to integrate the pressure-derived floor estimate with RSSI-derived location tracking. The aim was not to replace RSSI, but to use pressure as a vertical-location constraint when the strongest RSSI beacon appears to come from a different floor.

The combined method uses each signal for what it is best at:

| Signal | Role |
|---|---|
| Pressure | Estimate floor level |
| RSSI | Estimate nearest room/beacon within or across floors |
| ACC | Provide movement support for pressure floor shifts |

### Scripts

The Part 3 RSSI integration script is:

```text
src/SixthPhase/analyse_floor_aware_rssi_80h.py
```

It uses the 5-minute sensor feature table, the pressure-derived floor timeline, and the ACC support outputs from Part 2.

The floor mapping used in this analysis is:

### Pressure-Floor RSSI Rule

After Part 2 showed that the pressure floor timeline is generally supported by ACC, a more direct diagnostic rule was also tested:

> If the raw strongest RSSI beacon is on a different floor from the pressure-inferred floor, switch to the strongest RSSI beacon on the pressure-inferred floor.

This version does not use an additional pressure-confidence or ACC-support filter. It treats pressure as the main floor-level signal and RSSI as the within-floor proximity signal. This is intentionally direct: once pressure has provided the likely floor, the RSSI estimate is constrained to the strongest available beacon on that floor.

All switch counts below use only adjacent, continuously observed 5-minute beacon
windows. A change across an environmental-beacon gap is treated as an unknown
interval, not as a floor or room transition.

The raw strongest RSSI beacon disagreed with the pressure-derived floor in:

```text
55 / 848 five-minute windows = 6.49%
```

The outputs are:

```text
new80h_raw_vs_pressure_floor_bruteforce_rssi_two_line_timeline.png
new80h_pressure_floor_bruteforce_rssi_switch_summary.csv
```

![Raw and pressure-floor brute-force RSSI timeline](../Results/SixthPhase/NewData80h/new80h_raw_vs_pressure_floor_bruteforce_rssi_two_line_timeline.png)

The pressure-floor RSSI rule changed:

```text
54 / 848 five-minute windows = 6.37%
54 / 55 mismatched windows = 98.18%
```

One mismatched window could not be switched because there was no available RSSI candidate on the pressure-inferred floor in that 5-minute window.

### Beacon Floor Switch Versus Pressure Floor Switch

The pressure-derived floor timeline contains:

```text
28 floor switches
```

The raw strongest RSSI beacon floor is much noisier:

```text
75 raw RSSI beacon-floor switches
```

Only a minority of raw RSSI floor switches correspond to a pressure floor switch:

```text
Raw RSSI switch within +/-5 min of pressure switch: 28 / 75 = 37.3%
Raw RSSI switch within +/-10 min of pressure switch: 36 / 75 = 48.0%
Raw RSSI switch within +/-15 min of pressure switch: 40 / 75 = 53.3%
```

However, viewed from the pressure timeline, most pressure floor switches do have a nearby raw RSSI floor switch:

```text
Pressure switch covered by raw RSSI within +/-5 min: 24 / 28 = 85.7%
Pressure switch covered by raw RSSI within +/-10 min: 25 / 28 = 89.3%
Pressure switch covered by raw RSSI within +/-15 min: 26 / 28 = 92.9%
```

This suggests that raw RSSI often reacts near true floor changes, but also produces many additional cross-floor switches. Therefore, raw RSSI alone is too noisy for floor estimation.

After pressure-floor correction, the RSSI floor timeline becomes much more aligned with pressure:

```text
Pressure-floor-corrected RSSI floor switches: 30
Pressure-floor-corrected RSSI switch within +/-5 min of pressure switch: 28 / 30 = 93.3%
Pressure switch covered by pressure-floor-corrected RSSI within +/-5 min: 28 / 28 = 100.0%
Same-direction pressure switch coverage: 28 / 28 = 100.0%
```

### Stair-Proximity Beacon Check

An initial attempt was made to identify which beacon pair might be closest to the stairs by counting cross-floor beacon transitions in the pressure-floor-corrected RSSI timeline. The unordered cross-floor beacon-pair counts were:

```text
3E05 - CA59     8
CA59 - D7FD    10
1933 - 3E05     7
1933 - D7FD     5
```

This is not a strong enough pattern to identify a single stair-adjacent pair from transition counts alone. However, the two CA59 links to the upper-floor beacons remain close in frequency (8 with 3E05 and 10 with D7FD), which is consistent with the later feedback that the staircase sits between the two upper-floor rooms.

### Part 3 Interpretation

The Part 3 analysis supports the following interpretation:

* Pressure should be treated as the main floor-level signal.
* ACC supports the reliability of pressure-derived floor shifts.
* RSSI remains useful for nearest-beacon or room-level proximity, but raw RSSI floor switches are noisy.
* Pressure-floor gating can substantially reduce cross-floor RSSI ambiguity.
* The direct pressure-floor RSSI rule is a useful first combined method because it is simple, interpretable, and consistent with the Part 2 evidence that pressure floor shifts are generally movement-supported.

The practical conclusion is:

> Use pressure to determine the likely floor, then select or interpret RSSI beacons within that floor. Raw strongest RSSI should not be used directly as a floor signal because it produces many additional cross-floor switches that are not supported by the pressure timeline.
