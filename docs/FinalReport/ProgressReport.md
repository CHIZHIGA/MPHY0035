1.	Scientific context
Accurately measuring how people behave and function within their own homes is a core challenge in digital health research. Traditional clinical outcome measures and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) are inherently limited by subjectivity and episodic collection. Wearable actigraphy devices offer continuous activity data; however, isolated accelerometer measurements lack environmental context, making it difficult to interpret whether movement occurs in meaningful daily routines or specific rooms (e.g., bedroom vs kitchen). This limitation is particularly important in ageing and neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease and related disorders (ADRD), where functional independence and daily living patterns are key clinical endpoints.
Indoor localisation methods aim to bridge this gap by providing spatial context for activity data. Hill et al. (2024) showed that combining BLE beacon-derived room location with wearable activity data enables robust house-level monitoring with a mean room classification accuracy of 94.0% across multiple homes, demonstrating the value of context-aware functional measures over activity alone.
Indoor localisation technologies can be broadly categorised by sensing modality. Passive Infrared (PIR) wall-mounted sensors detect motion through changes in infrared radiation and are widely used in smart home and ambient assisted living research because of their low cost, low power usage, and privacy-friendly operation. PIR systems are effective for detecting motion events and broad occupancy but cannot resolve fine-grained room location or reliably distinguish between multiple occupants, and they do not capture activity intensity beyond binary motion detection.
Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) beacon systems represent an alternative approach for indoor positioning using Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI). BLE beacons transmit periodic signals that a wearable device can detect; RSSI values correlate approximately with distance, enabling proximity-based location inference. BLE-based solutions are attractive due to their low cost, low power, and widespread hardware support. Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated BLE’s feasibility for indoor positioning within homes, including hybrid systems combining BLE, PIR, and ultrasonic sensors, achieving high room detection accuracy (>95%) in residential scenarios when motion sensors were integrated with RSSI data. Bai et al. (2020) have developed BLE-based indoor positioning that uses trilateration and fingerprinting to track occupant location patterns, showing that BLE can support accurate long-term monitoring of daily living patterns for health-related applications.
Roohi et al. (2025) have highlighted the diversity of indoor positioning techniques and confirmed that RSSI-based approaches remain among the most widely studied due to their balance of simplicity, low cost, and deployment feasibility, particularly when combined with additional sensors or machine learning for improved accuracy and robustness.
The Panoramic Digital Health system (Hill et al.) used in this project deploys BLE environmental beacons in key rooms and a wearable bracelet worn by participants. Beacons broadcast signals approximately every 700 ms, and the bracelet records RSSI values with timestamps along with inertial sensor data (accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer) and environmental sensors (temperature, light, pressure). Location is estimated using a proximity-based algorithm where the beacon with the highest average RSSI within a defined time window is assigned as the current room. Minute-level estimates are subsequently aggregated using a sliding window (e.g., modal location over 15-minute periods) to smooth transient signal variations. This sliding-window highest-RSSI proximity classification algorithm is simple and requires no calibration, but its performance can be influenced by beacon placement and environmental signal variability.
Despite practical success, BLE RSSI is inherently noisy due to multipath effects, attenuation by walls and human bodies, and variable transmission environments, motivating research into improved RSSI processing, sensor fusion, and machine learning–based classification to increase localisation precision and contextual relevance. For example, Ceron et al. (2020) has shown promise for simultaneous pedestrian indoor localisation and environment mapping, providing both position estimates and movement context without external floorplans.
The present project builds on this foundation by integrating RSSI-based room localisation with wearable activity data, aiming to produce visualisation of two occupants’ spatiotemporal behaviour within a simplified home model. Combining proximity-based location with activity measures extends the system beyond basic localisation towards meaningful functional outcomes, addressing both “where” and “how active” a person is in each context.
2.	Project aims and objectives
2.1.	Research Question
This project seeks to address the following research question: How can indoor location and wearable sensor data be combined to produce an intuitive and clinically meaningful visualisation of the behaviour of two individuals within a home environment? The project builds on an existing validated Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) room-level localisation system and aims to extend its utility through integration with activity metrics and graphical representation.
2.2.	Overall Aim
The overall aim of this project is to develop and evaluate a visualisation framework that integrates RSSI-based indoor localisation with wearable-derived activity measures, enabling simplified yet meaningful representation of in-home behaviour for two occupants. This aim focuses not only on location estimation but on transforming raw digital signals into interpretable representations of daily functional behaviour.
2.3.	Specific Objectives
Objective 1: Develop and implement a baseline room-level localisation visualisation framework.
The first objective is to process raw RSSI data from environmental BLE beacons and apply a sliding-window highest-RSSI proximity classification algorithm to determine room-level location for two occupants. These location estimates are then mapped onto a simplified two-dimensional graphical representation of the home layout. The visualisation initially focuses on displaying spatial position only, enabling temporal tracking of both individuals simultaneously. Animated time-series outputs across multiple days are generated to demonstrate feasibility and validate clarity of spatial representation. This provides a structured foundation for interpreting spatial behaviour over time and establishes the basis for subsequent contextual analysis.
Objective 2: Derive and integrate an initial activity metric into the visualisation framework.
To incorporate behavioural context, a preliminary estimate of activity intensity is derived from cumulative wearable step-count data. Activity level within each 10-minute interval is calculated by subtracting cumulative step values, providing a simple sliding-window measure of movement. This metric is used to colour-code each individual within the home layout, enabling simultaneous visualisation of both location and activity intensity. Although step-count–based estimation provides a straightforward starting point, its limitations motivate further refinement using continuous acceleration signals. This step-count–based metric serves as an initial exploratory baseline, allowing comparison with more continuous acceleration-derived measures in later stages of the project.
Objective 3: Combine spatial and behavioural data into a unified dynamic representation.
A central objective of the project is to integrate room-level localisation and activity estimation into a coherent visual narrative of in-home behaviour. The system aims to present both where an individual is and how active they are within each context, extending beyond static location tracking toward functional interpretation. While room localisation and activity visualisation have been developed independently, full integration into a seamless unified representation is ongoing, and current progress demonstrates partial implementation of combined spatial and activity encoding.
Objective 4: Improve localisation robustness through sensor-fusion–based algorithm development.
The final objective is to enhance the baseline RSSI proximity algorithm by incorporating inertial sensor information from the wearable bracelet. Rather than relying solely on step-count–based measures, acceleration magnitude will be used as a continuous descriptor of movement intensity. By integrating RSSI data with accelerometer-derived motion information, the project aims to reduce transient misclassifications, improve temporal stability, and develop a more robust location estimation framework suitable for real-world domestic environments.
