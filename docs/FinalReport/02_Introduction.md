# 2. Introduction

Accurately understanding how people behave and function within their own homes is an important challenge in digital health research. Traditional clinical assessments and patient-reported outcomes are useful, but they are often episodic, retrospective, and affected by subjectivity. They may miss daily variation, routines, transitions, and the practical effects of disease on ordinary life [1]. Wearable and environmental sensors offer a complementary approach by recording movement and environmental context during normal daily activity, which is particularly relevant for ageing, dementia, Alzheimer’s disease and related disorders, and other long-term conditions where daily function and independence are clinically meaningful [2]. Wearable sensors can describe how active a person is, but activity data alone lack context [3]. Room-level location can make wearable activity data more meaningful by linking movement to functional spaces and daily routines [4]. This project therefore uses interpretable home-location estimation as a foundation for a broader clinical question: how can location, movement, and other environmental context be combined to better describe patient lived experience?

The method was developed iteratively, but the final analysis is organised as one unified movement-supported RSSI pipeline rather than a sequence of separate analyses. Early analyses identified recurring problems that the final framework needed to address: RSSI instability during low movement, missing RSSI during likely sleep, sensitivity of co-presence estimates to room correction, cross-floor ambiguity, and limited reference data. Early visualisations also clarified what the outputs should communicate: where a person appeared to be, whether they were active or settled, whether they appeared at home, and whether two people appeared together or separate.

The unified pipeline first audits the sensor and metadata available in each dataset and aligns the usable streams to a common five-minute timeline. Strongest RSSI provides the transparent raw location proposal. Movement state then controls how RSSI is stabilised, while sleep, away, missingness, and optional pressure evidence determine whether the proposal should be corrected, constrained, or left unresolved. Room, behaviour, and occupancy are retained as separate outputs. Optional branches add floor context and two-person co-presence without requiring these data sources in every collection.

This framework was applied across datasets with different sensor combinations and evidence levels. Earlier fixed-window, movement-adaptive, clustering, pressure, and night-time correction methods were retained as development evidence or dataset-specific comparators rather than presented as equal final methods. Labelled datasets supported quantitative reference agreement, while datasets without independent labels contributed descriptive stability, consistency, and plausibility evidence. This distinction is important because a more stable or complete timeline is not necessarily a more accurate one.

The aim of this project is therefore to develop and assess an auditable analysis framework and visualisation outputs that combine wearable sensor data, RSSI-derived environmental beacon data, and available environmental context to estimate home location and describe patient lived experience through activity-contextualised room use, co-presence, floor context, and behavioural summaries. Location estimates are treated as intermediate evidence rather than the final clinical outcome.

The project has five connected objectives. The first is to establish a reproducible capability audit and common five-minute workflow for aligning BLE RSSI, movement, optional pressure, metadata, and available reference annotations. The second is to retain a transparent strongest-RSSI room-level baseline within the unified pipeline. The third is to use movement, behavioural state, and pressure where available to interpret RSSI stability, transitions, and cross-floor ambiguity. The fourth is to extend location estimates into summaries of sleep, probable away, room transitions, floor context, and two-person co-presence. The fifth is to evaluate the framework using evidence appropriate to each dataset, including reference-annotation agreement, no-reference descriptive analysis, qualitative plausibility assessment, and comparison with earlier dataset-specific methods.
