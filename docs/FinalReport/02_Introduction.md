# 2. Introduction

Accurately understanding how people behave and function within their own homes is an important challenge in digital health research. Traditional clinical assessments and patient-reported outcomes are useful, but they are often episodic, retrospective, and affected by subjectivity. They may miss daily variation, routines, transitions, and the practical effects of disease on ordinary life [1]. Wearable and environmental sensors offer a complementary approach by recording movement and environmental context during normal daily activity, which is particularly relevant for ageing, dementia, Alzheimer’s disease and related disorders, and other long-term conditions where daily function and independence are clinically meaningful [2]. Wearable sensors can describe how active a person is, but activity data alone lack context [3]. Room-level location can make wearable activity data more meaningful by linking movement to functional spaces and daily routines [4]. This project therefore uses interpretable home-location estimation as a foundation for a broader clinical question: how can location, movement, and other environmental context be combined to better describe patient lived experience?
The aim of this project is to develop and evaluate analysis and visualisation methods that combine wearable sensor data, RSSI-derived environmental beacon data, and available environmental sensor context to estimate home location and describe patient lived experience through activity-contextualised room use, co-presence, floor transitions, and behavioural mobility summaries. This aim is broader than room localisation alone. Location estimates are treated as intermediate evidence that can support more interpretable summaries of daily behaviour.
The project has five connected objectives. The first is to build a reproducible preprocessing workflow for aligning BLE RSSI, movement, pressure where available, metadata, and reference labels. The second is to establish transparent RSSI-derived room-level localisation baselines. The third is to develop movement-aware and floor-aware methods that use activity and pressure context to interpret RSSI stability, transitions, and cross-floor ambiguity. The fourth is to extend location estimates into behavioural summaries, including co-presence, stair-transition candidates, likely sleep-location candidates, and active-period room-transition rates. The fifth is to evaluate methods using evidence appropriate to each dataset, distinguishing annotation agreement, no-reference descriptive analysis, reference-label accuracy, and qualitative plausibility assessment.
The work developed through an iterative analysis pathway. The earliest stage used annotation and activity outputs to explore location timelines, simplified floor-plan views, and early co-presence visualisations. This clarified what useful outputs should communicate: where a person appeared to be, whether they were active, and whether two people appeared together or separate. The next stage moved from processed annotations to raw RSSI and movement data in AA002, testing strongest-beacon baselines, low-motion filtering, adaptive RSSI windows, and RSSI stability features. 
The Home_X001 work extended the project to a two-person no-reference setting. The analysis aligned the two device streams over a shared period and used RSSI-derived room estimates to describe same-location, different-location, one-person-home, and away states. Because no independent reference labels were available, this part of the project was descriptive rather than accuracy-based. Its value was in showing how RSSI-derived location timelines could support interaction-aware representations of home behaviour, while showing how algorithm choice affected co-presence summaries.
Later work compared fixed-window RSSI, movement-adaptive RSSI, low-motion RSSI clustering, and a conservative hybrid method. The step-adaptive RSSI method used movement as temporal context, while clustering was treated as an exploratory way to identify repeatable RSSI signal states. Labelled datasets were then introduced to support stronger evaluation using accuracy, balanced accuracy, confusion matrices, and per-location recall.
The final stage extended the sensor-fusion approach beyond room-level localisation. In an 80-hour single-user two-floor dataset, pressure data from the wearable and environmental beacons were used to infer floor level. This pressure-derived floor estimate was combined with RSSI to reduce cross-floor ambiguity, and accelerometer features were used as supporting evidence for floor transitions. The resulting floor-aware location timeline was converted into preliminary behavioural metrics: stair-transition candidates, likely sleep-location candidates, and room-transition rates during active periods. This stage moved the project from sequences of estimated rooms toward interpretable behavioural summaries derived from multiple sensors.
