# 2. Introduction

Accurately understanding how people behave and function within their own homes is an important challenge in digital health research. Clinical assessments and patient-reported outcomes provide valuable information, but they are often collected intermittently and may not reflect the variability of everyday life [1]. Wearable and environmental sensors offer a complementary approach because they can collect information continuously during normal routines. For patients with long-term conditions, ageing-related functional change, or neurodegenerative disease, patterns of movement, rest, room use, stair use, and time spent together or apart may all contribute to a richer picture of lived experience [2].

This project is motivated by the gap between activity measurement and behavioural interpretation. Wearable motion sensors can describe how active someone is and when activity occurs [3], but movement alone does not explain where that activity takes place. A low-motion period, for example, may mean something different in a bedroom, living room, hallway, or outside the home. Room-level location can therefore make wearable activity data more meaningful by linking movement to functional spaces and daily routines [4].

Environmental Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) beacons provide one practical way to add this spatial context [5]. BLE-based indoor localisation has also been studied in non-medical settings such as indoor navigation, smart buildings, museums, campuses, retail, and asset tracking [6]. Therefore, this project does not claim to invent BLE localisation from first principles. Instead, it uses interpretable BLE localisation methods as a foundation for a broader question: how can RSSI-derived home location be combined with wearable movement and other environmental context to better describe patient lived experience?

The project also responds to a practical limitation of RSSI-based localisation: real homes are noisy sensor environments. RSSI signals can be affected by body occlusion, multipath propagation, beacon placement, missing detections, device orientation, and signal leakage between floors [7]. This means that location estimates should be interpreted with supporting context. Movement data can help identify stable and transitional periods, while pressure data can add floor-level context in multi-floor homes [8].

The aim of this project is to develop and evaluate analysis and visualisation methods that combine wearable sensor data, RSSI-derived environmental beacon data, and available environmental sensor context to estimate home location and describe patient lived experience through activity-contextualised room use, co-presence, floor transitions, and behavioural mobility summaries. This aim is broader than room localisation alone. Location estimates are treated as intermediate evidence that can support more interpretable summaries of daily behaviour.

The project has five connected objectives. The first is to build a reproducible preprocessing workflow for aligning BLE RSSI, movement, pressure where available, metadata, and reference labels. The second is to establish transparent RSSI-derived room-level localisation baselines. The third is to develop movement-aware and floor-aware methods that use activity and pressure context to interpret RSSI stability, transitions, and cross-floor ambiguity. The fourth is to extend location estimates into behavioural summaries, including co-presence, stair-transition candidates, likely sleep-location candidates, and active-period room-transition rates. The fifth is to evaluate methods using evidence appropriate to each dataset, distinguishing annotation agreement, no-reference descriptive analysis, reference-label accuracy, and qualitative plausibility assessment.

The work developed through an iterative analysis pathway. The earliest stage used existing annotation and activity outputs to explore location timelines, simplified floor-plan views, and early co-presence visualisations. This clarified what useful outputs should communicate: where a person appeared to be, whether they were active, and whether two people appeared together or separate. The next stage moved from processed annotations to raw RSSI and movement data in AA002, testing strongest-beacon baselines, low-motion filtering, adaptive RSSI windows, and RSSI stability features. It also established an important terminology distinction: comparison with an existing annotation file should be treated as agreement rather than ground-truth accuracy unless the annotation is independently verified.

The Home_X001 work extended the project to a two-person no-reference setting. The analysis aligned the two device streams over a shared period and used RSSI-derived room estimates to describe same-location, different-location, one-person-home, and away or unmapped co-presence states. Because no independent reference labels were available, this part of the project was descriptive rather than accuracy-based. Its value was in showing how RSSI-derived location timelines could support interaction-aware representations of home behaviour, while also showing how algorithm choice affected co-presence summaries.

Later work compared fixed-window RSSI, movement-adaptive RSSI, low-motion RSSI clustering, and a conservative hybrid method. The step-adaptive RSSI method used movement as temporal context, while clustering was treated as an exploratory way to identify repeatable RSSI signal states. Labelled datasets were then introduced to support stronger evaluation using accuracy, balanced accuracy, confusion matrices, and per-location recall.

The final stage extended the sensor-fusion approach beyond room-level localisation. In an 80-hour single-user two-floor dataset, pressure data from the wearable and environmental beacons were used to infer floor level. This pressure-derived floor estimate was combined with RSSI to reduce cross-floor ambiguity, and accelerometer features were used as supporting evidence for floor transitions. The resulting floor-aware location timeline was converted into preliminary behavioural metrics: stair-transition candidates, likely sleep-location candidates, and room-transition rates during active periods. This stage moved the project from sequences of estimated rooms toward interpretable behavioural summaries derived from multiple sensors.

The report is organised around this methodological and scientific progression rather than as a diary of stages. Chapter 3 provides the background on home monitoring, wearable activity sensing, BLE localisation, sensor fusion, behavioural metrics, and validation challenges. Chapter 4 describes the datasets and methods. Chapter 5 presents the results, moving from early visualisation and baseline localisation toward movement-aware methods, co-presence, labelled evaluation, and pressure-floor-aware behavioural summaries. Chapter 6 interprets the results in relation to the objectives, limitations, and prior work. Chapters 7 and 8 summarise the conclusions and future work.
