# 3. Background

The technical work in this project concerns how raw sensor streams can be converted into interpretable evidence about behaviour in the home. A low-movement period may indicate sleep, rest, sedentary behaviour, illness, device non-wear, or simply remaining in one room. The same step count may also have different meaning depending on whether it occurs in a bedroom, kitchen, living room, hallway, or outside the home. The background below therefore focuses on the sensing and localisation methods needed to add spatial and behavioural context to wearable activity data.
Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) beacons provide a practical way to estimate indoor location. A wearable device or smartphone can record Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) values from nearby beacons, and these readings can be used to infer proximity to rooms, zones, or coordinates [5]. BLE localisation has also been studied in non-medical settings, including museums, smart buildings, campuses, asset tracking, and indoor navigation [6]. Common BLE localisation approaches include proximity or nearest-beacon localisation, where the strongest beacon in a time window is mapped to a location. This method is transparent and calibration-light, making it useful as a baseline. More complex approaches include distance estimation with trilateration, RSSI fingerprinting, temporal filtering, and hybrid sensor fusion. These methods can improve performance in some settings, but they often require calibration, labelled data, and careful validation.
RSSI-based localisation remains difficult in real homes. During transitions, a time window may contain mixed evidence from multiple rooms. In multi-floor homes, a strong RSSI signal may also come from a beacon on another floor. For this reason, this project focuses on interpretable room-level and floor-aware estimates rather than precise coordinate tracking.
Sensor fusion is central to making these estimates useful. RSSI provides spatial evidence, movement data provides stability or transition context, and pressure data can provide vertical context where available. Movement is not treated as a direct room classifier; instead, low-motion periods can support longer RSSI windows, while active periods may require shorter windows. In two-floor homes, barometric pressure can help estimate floor level, and accelerometer evidence can support whether a pressure-derived floor change is likely to reflect movement rather than an artefact [7].
The project also considers social and behavioural context. In two-person homes, room-level estimates can be combined to describe whether two people appear to be in the same room, in different rooms, or whether one person appears away. These co-presence states may reflect shared routines, caregiving, independent activity, or separation, but they should be described as estimated co-presence rather than verified social interaction [4].
Location estimates are most useful when translated into behavioural summaries. A room timeline can show where a person may have been, but lived-experience interpretation often needs higher-level measures such as room-transition rates, stair-transition candidates, likely sleep-location candidates, and together/apart patterns.
These behavioural metrics require cautious interpretation. Pressure-derived floor transitions can suggest stair ascent or descent, but without independent observation they remain stair-transition candidates. Long low-motion night-time periods near a consistent beacon can suggest a likely bedroom candidate, but they do not prove sleep. Active-period room-transition rates may be more meaningful than raw transition counts because they account for how much time the participant appeared awake or moving.
Evaluation is a major challenge in real-world home sensing. Direct observation can provide detailed labels, but it reduces realism and is difficult to sustain in private homes. Video raises privacy concerns. Self-report and diary labels are more practical, but they may contain recall errors, coarse timing, and limited precision for short transitions or co-presence [1]. Existing annotation files may also be algorithm-derived rather than independent manual ground truth, so comparisons with them should be described as agreement rather than true accuracy.
Different datasets therefore support different evidence levels. Labelled datasets allow quantitative metrics such as accuracy, balanced accuracy, confusion matrices, and per-location recall. No-reference datasets require descriptive and credibility-based evaluation using transition counts, timeline stability, RSSI evidence, pairwise agreement, visual plausibility checks, and qualitative plausibility assessment. Overall, this project uses interpretable BLE localisation as a foundation, combines it with movement and pressure-derived context, and translates the resulting estimates into summaries closer to patient lived experience.
